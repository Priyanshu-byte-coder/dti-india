## 1. Project in brief

- **Paper:** "Measuring the Digital Task Intensity of Indian Occupations Using Large Language Models:
  Evidence on Wage Premiums and Labour Market Inequality from PLFS" — Anuja Nair (CSE), Gunjan Anand (HRM),
  Samik Shome (Economics), Nirma University; extended abstract submitted to SAU Digital Markets
  (`docs/extended_abstract.docx`). The paper must follow the abstract.
- **RQ1** Can an LLM reliably score the digital task content of Indian occupations from the official
  NCO-2015 descriptions? **RQ2** How does this India-specific measure differ from one transferred from
  the US O\*NET? **RQ3** What is the conditional wage premium of working in a more digital occupation
  (PLFS 2017-18 to 2023-24)? **RQ4** How does it differ by gender, education, rural/urban and formality?
- **Measure (DTI):** five dimensions scored 0–4 from the NCO text — D1 basic digital operation, D2 digital information processing, D3 digital communication, D4 specialised digital tools, D5 digital problem solving / content.
  DTI = equal-weight sum of the z-scored dimensions, re-standardised. Scored per 8-digit NCO-2015 occupation
  (3,450), averaged to 4-digit families and 3-digit groups (PLFS level), linked to NCO-2004 for pre-2021 rounds.
- **Instrument:** Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct (main) and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (second family), open-weight,
  run locally with llama.cpp on the Nirma PARAM Shavak GPU (Quadro GP100). Strict rubric: score only digital
  work the text names; silent → 0; every non-zero score needs a quote from the text. Five runs per occupation
  at temperature 0.7; median across runs. Prompt hash `46a323537a`.
- **Professors' decisions:** text only, no external sources; include the older PLFS rounds (NCO-2004) with a
  code conversion.

Cross-model and cross-rubric agreement in the pilot: strict (v1.0), llama8b_gguf vs qwen14b: Spearman 0.77, weighted κ 0.83; described-task (v1.1), llama8b_gguf vs qwen14b: Spearman 0.80, weighted κ 0.69; strict vs described, llama8b_gguf: Spearman 0.81; strict vs described, qwen14b: Spearman 0.86.

**Meaning.** The broader "described-task" reading barely reduced the all-zero share and lowered agreement between models, so the **strict rubric** is the main DTI (it is also the professors' literal answer and the rubric on the coder form). Qwen is more stable and better grounded than Llama, so **Qwen is the main instrument**.

**Meaning.** In the preferred model a 1-SD more digital occupation pays **4.1 log points** more (SE 1.3), below the
5.7 from the US-based measure. The estimate survives unweighted estimation, the wild cluster bootstrap, monthly earnings, direct
NCO links only and the Heckman correction; it is weaker in the pre-2021 rounds (10% level). It is non-linear (only the top two DTI fifths earn
more) and absent for casual workers. With the noisier Llama DTI it is 2.2 (p = 0.052): same pattern, attenuated.
Oster δ < 1 means modest selection on unobservables could explain the premium away, so it is reported as a **conditional occupational
association, not a causal return** (as in the abstract).

RIF = unconditional quantile regression (Firpo–Fortin–Lemieux). Conditional QR on a 100,000 subsample. The RIF estimates are the ones to interpret for "who gains across the distribution": nothing in the lower half, large at the top. The conditional quantiles answer a different question (position within one's own group of comparable workers): with the Qwen DTI they are larger at the bottom and zero at the top, with the O\*NET DTI they rise. The paper should lead with RIF and explain the difference.

**Meaning.** With India's own DTI the effect is 2.6–4.4 across learners; gradient boosting fits best
and gives 3.3 (p = 0.02). With the O\*NET DTI it ranges 3.4–6.9 and the best learner is not significant: the
US-based premium shrinks once controls enter flexibly. The causal forest named in the implementation plan has not been run (no R on the
server; `econml` would downgrade scikit-learn); the grf input is exported on the server.

### 5.7 Summary of RQ4 in words

- **Distribution:** no premium below the median; about 21 log points at the 90th percentile.
- **Education:** the clearest divide — zero for up-to-primary, positive for secondary and tertiary; within the same occupation graduates gain
  about 11–12 points more per SD than the least educated.
- **Formality:** among regular workers only those with social-security coverage gain; casual workers gain nothing.
- **Gender:** women's premium is larger than men's in every method, but DTI explains almost none of the ~32-point gender gap.
- **Rural–urban:** mixed (higher for rural workers when estimated separately, lower within occupations) — report as inconclusive.
- **Policy reading:** a *digital-returns gap* — occupational digital intensity pays for educated, formal, higher-paid workers, not for the
  less educated, informal and casual workforce.

## 6. RQ2 — India-specific vs O\*NET-transferred intensity

The same LLM and prompt (Qwen2.5-14B, strict rubric, 5 runs) scored 923 O\*NET occupations from their O\*NET description and core
tasks, mapped to NCO-2015 families through SOC → ISCO-08 with US employment weights. Comparison at the 4-digit family level
(361 families matched; 13 NCO families have no ISCO match, listed in `reports/gap/unmatched_codes.json`).

| O\*NET measure | Families | Spearman | Pearson |
|---|---|---|---|
| Share of core tasks with digital keywords | 360 | 0.49 | 0.55 |
| O\*NET Working with Computers | 360 | 0.61 | 0.60 |
| O\*NET Computers & Electronics knowledge | 360 | 0.57 | 0.60 |
| O\*NET e-mail frequency | 360 | 0.53 | 0.49 |

### 6.2 How large is the gap? (same instrument, 0–4 scale)

Mean dimension score per family: NCO-2015 text **0.47**, O\*NET text **1.18**; mean gap
(O\*NET − NCO) **+0.70**, positive for 80% of families. Families scoring 0 on the NCO text:
26%; on the O\*NET text: 15%.

| Division | Families | Gap, 0–4 scale (O\*NET − NCO) | Rank gap, same LLM (percentile points) | Rank gap, Muro measure |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Managers | 22 | +1.40 | +22 | +21 |
| 2 Professionals | 85 | +1.03 | +6 | +3 |
| 3 Technicians & assoc. prof. | 64 | +0.90 | -1 | +3 |
| 4 Clerical support | 25 | +0.88 | -2 | -1 |
| 5 Service & sales | 31 | +0.27 | -8 | +4 |
| 6 Skilled agriculture | 15 | +0.90 | +21 | +5 |
| 7 Craft & related trades | 61 | +0.35 | -5 | -5 |

| 9 Elementary occupations | 21 | +0.02 | -10 | -4 |

By skill level (0–4 scale gap): level 1 +0.02; level 2 +0.46; level 3 +0.90; level 4 +1.03; level Not defined +1.40.

Largest gaps (O\*NET text much more digital than the NCO text): Creative and Performing Artists Not Elsewhere Classified (+2.4); Information Technology Trainers (+2.4); Astrologers, Fortune Tellers and Related Workers (+2.4); Journalists (+2.2); Information and Communication Technology Services (+2.2); Personnel Clerks (+2.2); Business Service Agents Not Elsewhere Classified (+2.1); Mining Managers (+2.1).

**Meaning.** The two sources broadly agree on *which* occupations are digital (Spearman 0.68 with the same instrument,
0.49–0.61 with the conventional O\*NET measures), so the India-specific DTI is externally anchored. But the *level* differs sharply: read by the
same model with the same rubric, O\*NET descriptions of matching occupations score about 2.5 times as high as the
NCO-2015 descriptions. The gap is largest for managers, professionals, technicians, clerks and skilled agriculture (about +0.9 to +1.4 on the 0–4 scale) and close
to zero for elementary occupations. In relative (rank) terms O\*NET places managers and skilled agriculture higher than the Indian texts do, and
machine operators and elementary work lower. This is the RQ2 answer: transferring US task content to India overstates digital intensity,
especially in white-collar and managerial work. On its own it cannot separate two explanations (Indian jobs being less digital, or the 2015 NCO
texts describing work in less digital terms), which the paper should state. It fits the wage results: the O\*NET measure gives a larger
linear premium (5.7 vs 4.1) that is less robust to flexible ML adjustment (5.6).

## 8. Weak points a reviewer will raise

1. **What the index measures.** Half of occupations score 0 because the 2015 text rarely mentions digital work: the DTI partly measures how
   the classification was written. RQ2 and the coders' Q11 ("more digital in India today?") address this; an external check against an
   NSS survey with individual ICT skills would help most (to be checked whether it records occupation).
2. **No valid human validation yet** (3.5).
3. **Not causal:** Oster δ = 0.34.
4. **Coarse identifying variation:** ~230 occupation groups; equal weights within groups.
5. **Wages are nominal.** No state × sector CPI file was supplied, so the outcome is nominal (the variable name says "real");
   year-quarter fixed effects absorb common inflation and state FE permanent price differences. Add a CPI file and re-run, or state this.
6. **Weaker spots:** pre-2021 rounds (10% level), second LLM p = 0.052, Lasso/random-forest DML not significant at 5%,
   rural–urban mixed, 34% of occupations unstable on at least one dimension.
